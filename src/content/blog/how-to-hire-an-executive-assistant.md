Knowing **how to hire an executive assistant** is less about writing a clever job post and more about being clear on what you actually want the person to own. Most bad EA hires trace back to a fuzzy definition of the role, a rushed interview, and zero real-world testing before the offer. This guide walks the full process—define, scope, source, screen, interview, trial, and onboard—and shows where a managed service changes the calculus.

## Step 1: Define the role and the outcomes

Before you write a single job requirement, list the outcomes you want in six months. Not tasks—outcomes. "My inbox is triaged and I never miss a priority message." "Travel is booked and I just show up." "My week is planned and protected."

Outcomes tell you what kind of assistant you need. Someone who protects executive focus and exercises judgment is a different hire than someone doing pure data entry. Write down the 5 to 8 outcomes that would make the role obviously worth it. Everything downstream flows from this list.

## Step 2: Write the scope

Turn outcomes into a concrete scope. A good EA scope names the recurring responsibilities, the tools involved, the decision authority you are granting, and the boundaries.

Be explicit about authority. Can the EA decline meetings on your behalf? Spend up to a limit without asking? Reply to certain emails as you? Ambiguity here is where trust breaks down. Also state the working hours and time zone—if you need coverage across the U.S. business day, say so now.

## Step 3: Decide where to source

You have four realistic paths, each with tradeoffs.

| Source | Speed | Vetting burden | Cost model | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Job boards | Slow | All on you | Salary + overhead | In-office employees |
| Referrals | Varies | Mostly on you | Salary + overhead | Trust-first hires |
| Staffing agency | Medium | Shared | Markup or fee | One-off placements |
| Managed service | Fast | Handled for you | Flat monthly rate | Dedicated remote EA, fast |

Job boards and referrals give you maximum control but put all of the sourcing, screening, payroll, and replacement risk on you. ## Step 4: Screen and vet

Screening is where you filter for the non-negotiables before spending interview time.

- **Skills tests.** Give a short, realistic task—draft a reply to a tricky email, restructure a messy calendar, summarize a document. You are testing output quality, not trivia.
- **Scenario questions.** Ask how they would handle two urgent, conflicting requests, or an ambiguous instruction with the requester unreachable. You are watching for judgment and prioritization.
- **References.** Ask former managers specifically about reliability, discretion, and how much the person could be trusted to run without supervision.
- **Background checks.** For a role with access to your calendar, inbox, and possibly finances, a background check is basic hygiene.
- **Bilingual verification.** If your work spans English and Spanish, verify it live, not from a resume line.

## Step 5: Interview for judgment, not just polish

Charisma in an interview does not predict EA performance. Judgment does. Ask questions that reveal how someone thinks:

- "Tell me about a time you protected your executive's time by saying no to something. How did you decide?"
- "You notice a mistake in something your manager already sent to a client. What do you do?"
- "How do you decide what to escalate versus handle yourself?"
- "Walk me through how you'd take over my calendar in week one without breaking anything."

Listen for candidates who ask clarifying questions, weigh tradeoffs, and show they understand that an EA's job is to reduce your cognitive load, not add to it.

## Step 6: Run a paid trial task

This is the most predictive step in the entire process, and the one most people skip. Pay the finalist for a small, real slice of work—a few hours of inbox triage, planning a trip, building a simple tracker. A paid trial respects their time and shows you the thing no interview can: how they actually work when the task is real and the instructions are imperfect.

Watch for how they handle ambiguity, whether they communicate proactively, and whether the output needs heavy correction or lands close to right.

## Step 7: Onboard for the first 30 days

A great hire fails without onboarding. Plan the first 30 days deliberately.

**Week 1:** Grant access, walk through your tools and preferences, and start with low-risk tasks. Over-communicate. Record loom-style walkthroughs of recurring processes so the EA can build a playbook.

**Week 2–3:** Hand over full recurring responsibilities—calendar, inbox triage, travel—with a daily or twice-weekly check-in. Give direct feedback fast; small corrections now prevent bad habits later.

**Week 4:** Shift from directing to reviewing. The EA should be running the core scope with light oversight. Document what is working and where authority can expand.
