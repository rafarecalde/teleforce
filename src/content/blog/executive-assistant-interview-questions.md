Polish wins interviews. Judgment runs the job. If you are collecting **executive assistant interview questions**, start there, or you will hire the person who sounds composed and then pings you about every conflict on Thursday.

The gap shows up in week two. The polished candidate remembered your tools and your dog's name. The useful one looked at a double-booked afternoon, asked who the meeting was for, and declined the hold with no purpose. You will not hear that in "tell me about yourself." You will hear it in a messy prompt. You will see it in a short work sample.

Below is the question list and three tests you can run in a single sitting. The path from scope to offer lives in [how to hire an executive assistant](/blog/how-to-hire-an-executive-assistant). Use that for the process. Use this for the hour you are actually in the room.

## What the hour has to prove

Skip charm, software trivia, and the story about being the person who keeps everyone sane. Tools are a week of learning. You are testing four things.

- **Judgment with a missing fact.** Two decent options, one thing they do not know, a clock.
- **Discretion.** They notice something sensitive and do not turn it into a performance.
- **A system.** Loops die less often because of a habit they can describe without a slogan.
- **Writing.** The email is short, specific, and sounds like it has been sent before.

If your work moves between English and Spanish, test that live. A resume line is a claim. Two minutes of switching, on a real prompt, is the check.

Still unsure which job you are hiring for? [Executive assistant vs virtual assistant](/blog/executive-assistant-vs-virtual-assistant) is the split. A task-taker waits until the instruction is airtight. An EA makes a reasonable call and names the assumption. These questions are built to expose that split in under an hour.

## Executive assistant interview questions worth asking

Ask fewer than you planned, then leave silence. Polished candidates fill it with adjectives. Useful ones add a tradeoff, or they ask the question you forgot to include.

### Priorities

**1. A customer, an investor, and your cofounder all want the same hour tomorrow. You can reach me for two minutes. What do you do with those two minutes, and what do you do if you cannot reach me?**

A strong answer names the missing fact (crisis, or a hard deadline) and a default if you stay unreachable. "I would weigh the relationships" means the loudest person gets the hour.

**2. Someone important asks for a quick sync. There is no agenda. Walk me through the reply.**

You want a decline or a question you would send. "What decision are we making, and what should I read first?" is a working answer. "I would make it work" is how calendars fill up.

### Discretion

**3. A number in the deck does not match what I told the team yesterday. The meeting is in an hour. What do you do?**

Flag it privately. Do not guess a corrected figure. Do not raise it in the room before you have seen it. Better if they ask which number the company will stand behind.

**4. You are copied on a thread that shows a teammate is job-hunting. What do you do with that?**

Restraint. They do not gossip, and they do not perform how much they notice. If it touches a project they own, they ask you once, privately, whether you already know.

### Systems

**5. Tell me about a loop that used to die, and what you built so it stopped. What did your principal have to do?**

You want a Friday list, a day-three nudge, a column for who owes the next step. If they cannot name your part, the system is a diary. Every working one needs one behavior from you.

**6. How do you decide what to escalate?**

Push past "I use my judgment." Categories: money above a line, anything a customer will hear as a promise, legal, a surprise that would embarrass you in the meeting.

### The bad afternoon

**7. I am in back-to-backs. A vendor misses a date we already told a customer. What do you send, to whom, and what will you not promise?**

The draft should buy time without inventing a date, and tell you in one line what you owe. A novel is a miss. So is waiting until you emerge at 6 p.m.

**8. Describe a call you made on a principal's behalf that turned out wrong. What changed in the rule afterward?**

You want a scar and an edit to the rule. A candidate who has never been wrong has never held the pen.

If you only have 25 minutes, ask 2, 5, and 7. A decline, a system, and a draft. The rest is depth.

## Three work samples you can run in half an hour

Questions show how someone talks about judgment. A work sample shows the call they make while you stay quiet. Keep each one short. If you want half a day in a real inbox, pay for it.

Run them live, in a shared doc. Say the scenario is fictional and the instructions are incomplete on purpose. Then stop helping.

### Sample 1: Repair a Tuesday

Paste a fake day and ask for a proposed schedule plus the three questions they would ask before sending anything.

- 8:30 focus block, marked "writing"
- 9:30 vendor "quick sync," no agenda, they have asked twice
- 10:00 customer call you already accepted
- 10:00 investor update, overlapping the customer
- 11:30 interview you promised a candidate
- 12:00 lunch, empty
- 1:00 internal standup that has not produced a decision in a month
- 3:00 flight hold, still unconfirmed
- 4:30 "pick your brain" with a stranger a friend forwarded

Strong work protects the writing block or moves it on purpose, splits the 10:00 collision and names the assumption, kills or agendas the standup, and will not confirm a flight that is not real. Weak work color-codes the day and accepts every hold.

### Sample 2: Twelve subject lines, no bodies

Ask which three need you today, which they would handle, and which they would close without a reply.

- "Re: invoice 4418 is short"
- "Intro: would love to pick your brain"
- "Contract redlines, need you"
- "FYI logistics thread"
- "Candidate withdrew"
- "Can you jump on a call in 10?"
- "Board dinner, dietary restrictions"
- "Q3 numbers, please review before I send"
- "Following up on my last follow-up"
- "Password reset for the ads account"
- "Note from your family"
- "Warehouse says Thursday slipped"

Score the sort, and make them say the assumption. Redlines, the Q3 send, and a short invoice belong with you if those are your calls. "Pick your brain," the double follow-up, and the FYI do not. A family note gets a private flag. A password reset gets handled or routed. The warehouse slip needs a plan, not a forward with no recommendation.

### Sample 3: The nine-day nudge

A warm intro has sat for nine days. The last outbound line was "great to meet, I'll send times." Times were never sent. Have them write the nudge, then say what they do if there is no reply in 48 hours.

A useful nudge names the next step. "Putting 20 minutes on the calendar next week — Tuesday or Wednesday afternoon. If the timing is off, tell me and I will close the loop." A weak one says "just checking in" and stops. The 48-hour plan is one more note, then close it and tell you. "I would keep following up" fills an inbox with guilt.

## How to mark the hour

Mark each answer plainly. Did they ask one clarifying question before acting? Did they name a tradeoff? Could the writing go out with a light edit?

Choose the person whose work you would send. Passing on someone you liked is allowed. End it when they agree with every premise, will not decline anything, or talk about loyalty and cannot describe a system. You are hiring a vault with initiative, not a narrator.
