Most leaders don't struggle to find things to hand off. They struggle with the handoff itself. If you've ever thought "it's faster to just do it myself," you already understand why learning **how to delegate to an executive assistant** is the real skill—not finding one. A great EA can only give you back the hours you're willing to transfer, and transferring work well is a repeatable process.

This is a playbook, not a pep talk. Follow the steps below and you'll go from drowning to delegating in about 90 days.

## Start With What Drains You and Is Easy to Explain

The best first tasks sit at the intersection of two things: they cost you real time, and you can describe them in a few sentences. That usually means recurring, rules-based work—inbox triage, scheduling, travel, expense logging, follow-up reminders.

Avoid the temptation to start with your hardest, most judgment-heavy problem. You'll both get frustrated. Begin where the instructions are clear and the stakes are low, then expand. If you want a fuller menu, our guide to [tasks to delegate to an executive assistant](/blog/tasks-to-delegate-to-an-executive-assistant) breaks it down category by category.

A simple test: if you catch yourself doing something for the third time this month and it doesn't require your specific expertise, it's a delegation candidate.

## Give Context, Not Just Instructions

The difference between a mediocre handoff and a great one is context. Instructions tell your EA *what* to do. Context tells them *why*, which lets them make good decisions when reality doesn't match your script.

Compare these two handoffs:

- **Instruction only:** "Book me a flight to Chicago on the 14th."
- **Instruction plus context:** "Book me a flight to Chicago on the 14th. I have a client dinner at 7 p.m., so I need to land by 3 p.m. with buffer. I prefer aisle seats, avoid the last flight out in case of delays, and my client relationships matter more than saving $80."

The second version lets your EA solve problems without pinging you. Context is what turns an order-taker into a thought partner. Every time you explain the "why," you're training judgment you won't have to supply again.

## Build Living SOPs and Checklists

Say it once, then write it down. The first time you delegate a recurring task, have your EA draft a standard operating procedure (SOP) as they learn it. You review it; they own it. Now the process lives in a document instead of your head.

Good SOPs are living documents. When an edge case comes up, the fix goes into the SOP so it never surprises anyone again. Over a few months you accumulate a library that makes your EA more valuable—and makes you replaceable in the best possible way.

Have your assistant maintain these, not you. Writing and updating SOPs is itself a task to delegate.

## Use One Shared Task Tool and a Weekly Rhythm

Delegation breaks down when work lives in scattered texts, emails, and hallway asks. Pick one shared task tool—Asana, Trello, Notion, a shared list, whatever you'll actually use—and route everything through it. Your EA should never have to guess where the next task is coming from.

Then set a rhythm. A short weekly planning call and a brief daily async check-in cover most needs:

| Cadence | Purpose | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly planning call | Priorities, upcoming week, blockers | 20–30 min |
| Daily async update | Status, questions, what's next | 5 min written |
| Monthly review | What to expand, what to hand off next | 30 min |

## Let It Be a B+ the First Time

Here's the mindset shift that unlocks everything: your EA's first attempt does not need to be exactly how you'd do it. It needs to be good enough, and better next time.

If you reject anything that isn't a perfect A, you'll spend more energy correcting than you save—and you'll teach your assistant to check with you on everything. Accept the B+, give one or two pieces of specific feedback, and let the SOP absorb the lesson. Quality climbs on its own when you coach instead of redo.

Perfectionism is the number-one reason smart people fail at delegation. Don't let it be yours.

## How Trust and Scope Compound: 30/60/90

Delegation is a compounding investment. Each task you successfully hand off frees time *and* builds the shared context that makes the next handoff faster.

- **Days 1–30:** Recurring, low-judgment tasks. Inbox, calendar, travel, expenses. Heavy SOP-building. Frequent check-ins. You're teaching; your EA is documenting.
- **Days 31–60:** Multi-step projects with more ambiguity. Research briefs, vendor coordination, CRM upkeep, drafting communications for your review. Fewer check-ins; more ownership.
- **Days 61–90:** Judgment-based work. Inbox managed to zero on your behalf, calendar owned outright, projects run end-to-end, your EA anticipating needs before you name them.

By day 90, a well-onboarded assistant isn't waiting for tasks—they're removing them from your plate before you notice.

## A Simple Week-1 Delegation Plan

If you want somewhere concrete to start, run this in your first five days:

1. **Day 1:** Grant access—email, calendar, task tool, key logins via your password manager. Walk through your top three time drains.
2. **Day 2:** Hand off inbox triage and calendar management. Have your EA start the SOPs as they go.
3. **Day 3:** Add travel and expense logging. Do one task together, then let them run the next solo.
4. **Day 4:** Introduce a small research or coordination task with full context. Review the output; accept the B+.
5. **Day 5:** Hold your first weekly planning call. Agree on priorities and the daily check-in format.

That's it. You don't need a perfect system on day one—you need to start the loop of delegate, review, document, and expand.

## The One Habit That Makes It Work

Delegate consistently, even when you're busy—*especially* when you're busy. The instinct under pressure is to grab tasks back and do them yourself. That's exactly when handing them off matters most. Every task you keep is a lesson your EA never gets to learn.

Learning how to delegate to an executive assistant is less about technique and more about repetition. Give context, accept imperfect first drafts, document as you go, and watch your capacity expand month over month.
