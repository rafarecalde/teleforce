Gallup’s onboarding research is blunt: new employees typically take **around 12 months** to reach full performance in a role. Most companies still treat onboarding as a first-week orientation. Only a small share of employees say their organization does it well.

That is the wrong clock for an executive seat. You are not hiring a branch manager. You are buying an owner for the interruption layer. The first 30 days with a virtual EA should ship visible loops — a filtered inbox, a defended calendar, two meetings that arrive briefed — not a shadowing period that ends when you have forgotten why you hired.

The failure mode is almost never talent. It is a principal who keeps the keys.

## What to finish before day one

Do this before kickoff, not the morning they start. Teleforce’s [EA offer](/) covers this on the onboarding call, then sends a short written plan and an access checklist within 24–48 hours. Fill the gaps yourself.

**1. A one-page decision rule.** What they always take. What they close. What they draft for you. People who skip the line. Tone notes (“short, no exclamation points, never apologize for a delay we caused”). This is the document [what a virtual executive assistant actually does](/blog/virtual-executive-assistant) assumes you will write and almost nobody does.

**2. Access, not a tour.** Calendar with scheduling authority — not view-only. Inbox. The Slack or Teams channels where work actually happens. CRM or project tool. Travel card or expense path. A password manager, not a screenshot dump.

**3. A live queue, not a wish list.** Ten open loops with a name and a date. The two recurring meetings they will own first. The one ugly chase that has been sitting on you for weeks.

**4. A pulse on the calendar.** Fifteen minutes at the end of each of the first five days. Then twice a week. If you will not show up, do not hire.

If those four things are missing, you are not onboarding an EA. You are hoping.

## The first 30 days with a virtual EA, week by week

This is a handoff sequence, not a personality plan. Steal it.

### Days 1–7: access, shadow, close something small

They sit in the inbox and the calendar with you. You narrate *why* you accept, decline, or ignore — not just what you click. They take notes. You do not write a novel.

By Friday they should have:

- Closed or filed a stack of FYIs you would have deleted anyway
- Booked or moved at least one meeting to your rules
- Drafted two replies you actually sent
- Written back the decision rule in *their* words so you can correct it

Daily pulse: what they did, what is blocked, what they need from you. Keep it to fifteen minutes. If it runs to an hour, you are still the integrator.

### Days 8–14: two workstreams, fully theirs

Pick calendar defense *and* inbox triage together. They are one system. Splitting them creates collisions — a meeting booked against a thread they never saw.

Give them two recurring meetings. Brief the day before. Recap and owners the same day. When that loop is boring, you have a seat. When you still write the recap, you have a note-taker.

This is also when [how a virtual EA frees up time](/blog/how-virtual-ea-frees-up-time) becomes measurable: you stop being the person who chases the designer, the lawyer, or the warehouse.

### Days 15–21: one ugly chase, plus travel or research

Hand them the thing you have been “just handling.” A software renewal. A vendor who wants a revised date. An invoice finance will bounce. Watch whether they close it without you on every email.

Add one bounded research brief or one trip to your rules. Finite work trains trust faster than “be proactive.”

### Days 22–30: expand or diagnose

If the scoreboard below is mostly green, add project tracking or a weekly ops closeout. If it is red, do not add scope. Fix access, rules, or the habit of re-doing their work.

A 30-day review is a working session, not a vibe check. Bring the scoreboard. Name the next two workstreams — or the rematch.

| Signal | Green by day 30 | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Inbox | You open a must-act list, not the firehose | You still archive the FYIs |
| Calendar | Focus blocks survive most weeks | They book; you still defend |
| Meetings | Two briefs arrive without you asking | You write the recap |
| Loops | Someone else nudges the quiet person | You are still the chase |
| Access | They can act without pinging you for logins | View-only calendar, shared passwords in chat |

## The five ways principals stall their own EA

Say these out loud. They are common. They are on you.

**1. View-only access.** An assistant who cannot move a hold cannot own the week.

**2. Re-doing the work.** If you rewrite every draft, they will stop drafting. Correct the rule once. Then stay out of the closed bucket for a week.

**3. No pulse.** Same-day overlap is wasted if they cannot get ten minutes to unblock. Nearshore hours only help if you use them.

**4. Dumping a broken process.** They will faithfully run a mess. Kill the meeting that has no decision before you staff it.

**5. Hiring a task pack and expecting a chief of staff.** Hour-pack VAs do not become owners on day 30. A dedicated seat can. Do not mix the models and blame the person.

In-house onboarding has the same failure modes plus a requisition and a notice period. If you are still deciding the model, the [virtual EA vs. in-house](/blog/virtual-ea-vs-in-house) scorecard is the sibling. This post assumes you already bought a seat.

## What “good” looks like on day 31

You should be able to answer yes without staring at a timesheet:

- Mornings start from a short list they built
- At least two recurring meetings arrive briefed
- One vendor or teammate is being chased by someone who is not you
- A calendar hold died because it had no purpose — and you did not kill it
- You took a customer, product, or fundraising block that used to get eaten

You will not have 12-month “full performance.” You should have a working operating layer. Gallup’s year-long ramp is what happens when nobody writes the rules and nobody hands over the tools. A dedicated EA is the opposite bet: concentrate the first month on two systems and one chase, then expand.
