Most companies spend more time debating *whether* to go nearshore than they spend actually vetting the vendor they choose. That imbalance is how costly misfires happen.

McKinsey research found that organizations score an average of 3.0 out of 5 when evaluating outsourcing vendors upfront — but only 2.5 out of 5 on ongoing supplier management after contracts are signed. The selection process gets most of the attention; the governance that keeps a partnership healthy gets half as much. Knowing how to choose a nearshore support provider means building the rigor into *both* phases, before and after signature.

This post gives you a practical due-diligence framework: six checks, in priority order, with the questions you should actually ask.

## Why the stakes are higher with nearshore than offshore

Offshore relationships are often transactional by design — high volume, standardized tickets, low switching cost. Nearshore is usually chosen because you need something harder to replicate: real-time collaboration, bilingual fluency, cultural nuance, brand voice. That makes a bad nearshore match more damaging. You've paid a premium over offshore rates and still got a poor outcome.

The good news: a rigorous vetting process eliminates most of the risk. The bad news: most buyers skip at least two of the six checks below.

## The six due-diligence checks

### 1. Time-zone and coverage model

Start with the operational basics. Verify that the provider's actual staffing hours — not their advertised coverage window — overlap with your peak contact volume. Ask for a sample weekly schedule, not a brochure.

Questions to ask:
- What percentage of agents are on shift during U.S. Eastern 9 a.m. – 6 p.m.?
- How is overflow handled during spikes (holidays, product launches)?
- Is weekend staffing included, or a separate add-on?

For most U.S. companies, Latin American nearshore providers operate within one to three hours of your team. That's the value — confirm it's actually available on your contract terms, not just "possible."

### 2. Language fluency — verified, not assumed

This is where vetting breaks down most often. Nearshore providers universally claim strong English and Spanish fluency. Few buyers verify it before signing.

Ask for:
- **Recorded call samples** across ticket types (billing, technical, complaint resolution), not cherry-picked showcase calls.
- The provider's **QA rubric** for language scoring — what constitutes a passing score and how often agents are evaluated.
- **Attrition data by language tier** — high attrition in the top fluency tier is a red flag regardless of how good the average sample sounds.

If bilingual support is a core requirement, [testing an agent's Spanish fluency](/blog/test-agent-spanish-fluency) is a step you cannot skip.

### 3. Infrastructure and security posture

"Enterprise-grade security" is a phrase, not a certification. Ask for documentation.

Minimum baseline:
- **PCI DSS** compliance if you handle payment data
- **SOC 2 Type II** report (not Type I — Type II covers operational continuity over time)
- **GDPR/CCPA** data-processing addendum ready to review
- Redundant internet connectivity (dual-ISP minimum) and a documented disaster-recovery plan

Also ask: what tooling do they already integrate with? A provider who's never connected to Zendesk, Salesforce, or Interforce will need ramp time — and that ramp time comes out of your SLA performance window.

### 4. Pricing model transparency

The number in a proposal and the number on your invoice six months in are not always the same. Understand the full cost structure before you sign.

Common hidden costs in nearshore BPO contracts:
- Training hours billed at full agent rate
- Supervisor or QA overhead passed through as a percentage
- "Peak surcharges" for seasonal volume that weren't defined in the base SLA
- Technology fees for integrations that were implied as included

[Understanding your real support cost baseline](/blog/cost-of-in-house-customer-support) before you negotiate gives you an honest comparison point — not just a vendor's favorable math.

### 5. References — specific, not generic

Every nearshore provider will share references. The question is whether those references are meaningful to your situation.

Ask for references from clients who:
- Are in the same industry or handle similar ticket complexity
- Were at your approximate volume when they started (not clients who scaled to 10x your size)
- Have been live for at least 12 months (anyone can look good in a 90-day honeymoon period)

When you speak to references, the most useful questions are operational and unglamorous: How did the provider handle a bad month? What did ramp actually look like vs. what was promised? How responsive is account management when something goes wrong?

### 6. Offboarding and data portability

The last thing you want to think about when you're evaluating a new partner is how you'd leave them. Think about it anyway.

A reputable provider will not resist a clear offboarding clause. It should specify:
- Timeline for returning call recordings, ticket histories, and agent training data
- Data destruction certification for anything not returned
- Non-solicitation protection for agents trained on your brand's processes
- A knowledge transfer plan that prevents a cliff-edge quality drop if you switch

The providers who push back hardest on offboarding terms are often the ones with the weakest retention confidence.

## The comparison table: what good looks like vs. what's acceptable

| Check | Green flag | Yellow flag |
|---|---|---|
| Time-zone overlap | Documented shift schedule, live weekends | "We can arrange coverage" without specifics |
| Language fluency | QA rubric, recorded samples, attrition data by tier | Marketing demo call only |
| Security | SOC 2 Type II, PCI DSS cert, DR plan | "We take security seriously" |
| Pricing | Fully itemized, surcharge conditions defined | Base rate only, details TBD |
| References | Same industry, 12+ months live | Logo list without contact info |
| Offboarding | Clear written clause | Resistance or vague language |
