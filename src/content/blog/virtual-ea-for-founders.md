A virtual EA for founders is not a junior version of the Fortune-500 brief. It is a different job that happens to share a title.

Prialto’s 2025 Executive Productivity Report surveyed 508 leaders and founders and found the same leak in different offices: **more than half of executives spend 30% or more of the day on busywork**, and the typical leader still loses **8–12 hours a week** to work someone else could own. The report’s quieter line is the one that matters for staffing: founders and small-business owners lack the support systems their larger counterparts already take for granted.

Enterprise ops already has lanes — a chief of staff, department coordinators, an in-house assistant on the floor. The virtual seat fills a gap. A founder *is* the gap. If you buy the enterprise brief for a six-person company, you will under-use a specialist. If you buy the founder brief for a 400-person company, you will drop a generalist into a political system they cannot see.

## The stage test: who else owns the week?

Ask one question before you write a job description.

**If this person disappeared on Tuesday, who runs the calendar and the open loops?**

- **You.** You are in the founder pattern. One dedicated EA should sit next to you — not next to “the company.”
- **A chief of staff, an in-house EA, or an ops manager.** You are in the enterprise pattern. The virtual seat needs a lane, a named principal, and a rule about what they do *not* touch.
- **Nobody, and five people think they are the principal.** You are not ready. Write ownership first. Then hire.

This is the same honesty as [virtual EA vs. in-house](/blog/virtual-ea-vs-in-house): model follows the work, not the brochure.

## What a virtual EA for founders should own in the first quarter

A founder EA is chief-of-staff-lite. They are not your head of people, your closer, or your therapist. They are the person who keeps the company from routing every interrupt through you.

**Own these:**

- Calendar and inbox as one system — filter, focus blocks, meeting prep
- Open loops across a small team: who owes what, by when
- Vendor and contractor chase (legal, bookkeeping, tools, the landlord)
- Travel and the expense packet
- A weekly closeout you can read in four minutes
- Light hiring support: scheduling, packets, the thank-you you will not send
- Bilingual threads if your vendors or customers need English and Spanish without you on the call

**Do not dump these:**

- The actual customer, investor, or product conversation
- Comp, equity, and anything that creates a company obligation you have not written down
- A full outbound engine. That is [appointment setting](/services/appointment-setting) or an SDR seat, not “EA plus a dialer”
- Being the shared assistant for every cofounder, the board, and the office dog

The [first 30 days with a virtual EA](/blog/first-30-days-virtual-ea) still apply: access, a decision rule, two meetings, one ugly chase. Founders skip the rule because “we’re informal.” Informal is how you become the bottleneck again by week three.

Prialto’s same 2025 survey found AI use among executives at 78%, and full trust at 26%. Founders feel that gap daily. Drafts are cheap. Someone who will decline a meeting *in your name* is not. Hire for the trust gap, not for another tool.

## What enterprise ops should buy instead

A 200-person company that says “we need a VA” usually needs one of three things. Name which.

**1. A lane next to a senior in-house EA.** Inbox triage, vendor volume, bilingual supplier threads, travel packets, the weekly ops recap. The in-house hire keeps the hallway and the board dinner. The virtual hire keeps the queue off the executive. Write the swim lane on day one. One person owns the live calendar. Review it in 30 days.

**2. A seat for a specific principal.** A VP of sales, a COO, a founder who still runs a function. Dedicated, named, full-time. Not a pool that “the leadership team” shares. Shared pools become ticket queues. Ticket queues become your problem again.

**3. A different function.** If the pain is a support backlog, data cleanup, or a meeting pipeline, buy that seat. Do not dress an EA as a department.

Enterprise failure modes are predictable:

- Two assistants, no lane
- An EA asked to “support the ELT”
- A marketplace freelancer treated as a confidential chief of staff
- A virtual hire with view-only access because legal has not finished the memo

If most of the work must be in the room, build locally. If most of it is digital and you need speed or Spanish, buy the seat.

| | Founder / small ops | Enterprise ops |
|---|---|---|
| Principal | One founder, maybe a cofounder with a hard line | One named exec, not “the team” |
| Scope | Generalist: week + loops + vendors | Specialist lane next to existing ops |
| Risk | You never delegate | Politics and duplicate owners |
| Wrong buy | Hour-pack VA you still manage | Shared pool labeled “EA” |
| Right buy | One dedicated FTE on your hours | Dedicated FTE with a written lane |

## When the founder pattern should stay founder-sized

Some companies should not “grow into” an enterprise EA structure.

If you are still under a dozen people and the founder is the product, the fundraise, and the closer, one strong generalist is the whole design. Adding a second EA before you have a second principal is how you invent busywork.

If you are adding the second seat because the first is drowning, check the brief before you check the headcount. They may be running sales ops, recruiting, and the inbox. Split the *function*, not just the person.

If you will not grant inbox access, neither model works. You will get a scheduler. Call it that.
