The virtual EA vs in-house debate gets framed as cheap versus premium. That is the wrong axis.

The right axis is *what the job needs to be in the room for.* If the answer is “almost never,” buying a dedicated remote seat is usually faster and cleaner than opening a requisition. If the answer is “most afternoons,” stop interviewing virtual candidates and hire locally.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics’ 2024–34 outlook is the quiet tell. Employment of executive secretaries and executive administrative assistants is projected to *decline slightly* (about 1.6%) over the decade, even as the broader secretary and administrative-assistant group still generates hundreds of thousands of openings a year — almost all of them replacement, not growth. BLS points to managers absorbing more of the work themselves and to software, including AI, handling routine pieces.

Read that as an operator, not as a labor economist: the *need* for an owner of the week did not shrink. The domestic headcount did. A lot of that work landed back on founders and chiefs of staff who already cannot get a morning. The build-vs-buy question is how you put an owner back in the chair without pretending 2014 hiring is still the default.

## Virtual EA vs. in-house: a practical scorecard

Score yourself honestly. Do not score the brochure.

| Dimension | In-house EA | Dedicated virtual EA |
|---|---|---|
| Time to a productive seat | Recruit, offer, notice period, ramp — often months | Match and onboard in days to a couple of weeks |
| Presence | In the room, on the floor, at the dinner | On the tools, on the clock, not in the lobby |
| Employer of record | You (payroll, benefits, unemployment, management) | Provider employs; you get a monthly seat |
| Language | Whatever you can hire in your city | Bilingual English/Spanish is a design choice, not a unicorn search |
| Hours | Your office hours, plus whatever commute does to energy | Can be aligned to U.S. business hours from Latin America |
| Confidentiality | Strong for physical files and side-of-desk conversations | Strong for digital work if access and NDAs are real; weak if you refuse to grant tools |
| Backup | You recruit again when they leave | A rematch is part of a managed seat |
| Brand / hospitality | Hosts visitors, reads the room | Does not replace a front-of-house hire |

This is the same shape as the [outsourced vs. in-house support](/blog/outsourced-vs-in-house-support) decision: speed, control, language, and volume — not a single cost cell.

## When in-house wins

Say it plainly. In-house is the right call when:

- **The job is physical.** Badge access, a reception presence, printing and binding for a board that still wants paper, walking a guest to the conference room.
- **The work is classified or soil-bound.** Your counsel or a customer contract says the assistant must be a U.S. employee on U.S. soil. Do not lawyer that away in a blog post — just obey it.
- **You already have a world-class EA and a working office.** Do not rip that out for a thought piece. Add a virtual seat for overflow if you need one.
- **You will not grant system access to anyone off-site.** Then a virtual hire is theater.

If three of those are true, build. Teleforce is not the answer to a front-desk problem.

## When a virtual seat wins

A dedicated virtual EA — not a marketplace freelancer you babysit — wins when:

- **You need the seat this quarter.** A [virtual executive assistant](/blog/virtual-executive-assistant) who already works this way ramps on your tools, not on “finding a two-bedroom near the office.”
- **Your market and vendors are bilingual.** One person who can run English and Spanish threads without a handoff. That is hard to hire in most U.S. cities and expensive when you do.
- **You do not want to become an employer of record abroad.** Nearshore talent on a managed seat means payroll, HR, and rematch stay with the provider.
- **The work is digital.** Inbox, calendar, research, travel, vendors, CRM hygiene, follow-ups. That is most of the modern EA job.
- **You have been absorbing the BLS shift yourself** — doing your own scheduling and chase-downs “until we hire” — and the until never comes.

## The hybrid that actually works

Plenty of companies should not pick a side.

Keep a senior in-house EA next to a CEO who lives in the office. Add a virtual EA to own volume: inbox triage, vendor follow-up, bilingual supplier threads, travel packets, the weekly ops recap. The in-house hire keeps judgment and the hallway. The virtual hire keeps the queue from climbing back onto the executive.

The failure mode is two people with unclear lanes. Write the swim lane on day one. One owns the principal’s live calendar. The other owns a named workstream. Review it in 30 days.

The other hybrid: you are not ready for an EA at all. You need [appointment setting](/services/appointment-setting) or a support queue. Different seat. Do not dress an SDR up as a chief of staff.

## Freelancer, marketplace, or employed seat

“Virtual” is not one model.

**Freelance / marketplace.** Fast to try. You are the manager, the backup plan, and the compliance owner. Fine for a bounded project. Fragile as the person next to a CEO.

**Hour packs and ticket VAs.** Cheap feelings. No one owns the week. You stay the integrator.

**Employed, dedicated seat.** The provider recruits, pays, coaches, and rematches. You interview and select. This is the Teleforce model: LATAM talent, U.S. hours, English and Spanish, Fortune 500-grade delivery standards behind a 30-year operator. You are not hiring “a VA from the internet.” You are buying a named FTE without running the foreign entity.

## A 30-day decision test

You do not need a six-month pilot to know.

1. List the workstreams from [what a virtual EA actually does](/blog/virtual-executive-assistant).
2. Mark each **must be in-person** or **can be digital**.
3. If most rows are digital and you need bilingual or speed, buy the seat.
4. If most rows are in-person, open the req.
5. If the list is mostly “I will know it when I see it,” you are not ready to hire anyone. Write the rules first.
