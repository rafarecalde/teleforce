Most people quote an executive assistant cost by naming a salary. That number is almost always wrong—and always too low. The **executive assistant cost** you actually pay includes benefits, payroll taxes, software, recruiting fees, management overhead, and the hours you lose when the seat is empty or the person is out. Add it up and the sticker price roughly doubles.

This post breaks down the fully loaded numbers three ways: a U.S. in-house full-time EA, a U.S. part-time or agency EA, and a dedicated nearshore EA. Then it reframes the whole question around the only number that really matters—what your own hour is worth.

## The fully loaded executive assistant cost, line by line

When you hire a U.S. in-house executive assistant, the base salary is the beginning of the bill, not the end. Here is what stacks on top.

**Base salary.** A capable full-time EA in a mid-to-large U.S. metro runs roughly $65,000 to $85,000 a year. In high-cost cities, senior EAs go well past that.

**Benefits.** Health insurance, retirement match, paid time off, and other benefits typically add 25% to 35% on top of salary. On an $75,000 salary, that is $18,000 to $26,000.

**Payroll taxes.** Employer-side Social Security, Medicare, and unemployment taxes add roughly 8% to 10%—another $6,000 to $7,500.

**Tools and equipment.** Laptop, phone, scheduling software, password managers, and other subscriptions run $1,500 to $3,000 a year.

**Recruiting.** Whether you use a recruiter (15% to 25% of first-year salary) or spend your own weeks screening, sourcing a good EA has a real cost—often $10,000 to $18,000 amortized over the hire's tenure.

**Management and downtime.** You will spend your own time managing the role. And when your EA is on vacation, out sick, or leaves, coverage is your problem. The average U.S. tenure for administrative roles is a few years, so recruiting is a recurring line, not a one-time one.

Put together, a U.S. in-house EA who "costs $75,000" really costs **$95,000 to $120,000 a year**, or roughly $8,000 to $10,000 a month.

## The ROI framing: your hour vs. the EA rate

Cost is only half the equation. The other half is what you free up.

Work out your effective hourly value. If you run a business or lead a team and your time is worth, conservatively, $150 an hour, then every hour spent booking travel, chasing invoices, formatting decks, or triaging your inbox is a $150 hour spent on $30 work.

A dedicated EA typically takes 10 to 15 hours a week off your plate once ramped. At $2,700 a month, the Teleforce rate works out to roughly $17 per hour of assistant time on a full-time schedule. If that hour buys back an hour of your $150 time, the return is not close—it is close to 9x on the reclaimed hours alone, before counting the higher-leverage work you can now do.

The break-even is low. Even if your time is worth $75 an hour and the EA only reclaims 8 focused hours a week, the delegated hours cover the cost several times over.

## Be honest about the tradeoffs

A nearshore dedicated EA is not the right fit for every task. Some work genuinely needs a physical presence—signing for packages, staffing a front desk, running local errands. For those, no remote model works, in-house or nearshore.

There is also a ramp period. Any new assistant, in any model, needs a few weeks to learn your preferences, tools, and judgment calls. The difference is who carries the recruiting and replacement risk. With in-house, that risk is yours. With a managed service, it is absorbed into the flat rate.
